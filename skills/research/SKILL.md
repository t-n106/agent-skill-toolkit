  Build a trustworthy evidence set from external or internal sources when facts

# Research

## Task Boundary

Respect any authority, scope, role, and constraints already established by the user, host environment, or routing/orchestration layer. If none are explicitly defined, treat the current task context as the working boundary.

This skill does not expand that boundary unless the governing context explicitly authorizes it.

---

## Purpose

Use this skill to determine **what the available evidence actually supports**.

Research owns evidence acquisition and source assessment.

It does not own:

- product requirement authority;
- final decision authority;
- broad analysis beyond source support;
- implementation;
- automatic acceptance of source claims.

Primary rule:

> Gather the smallest sufficient body of trustworthy evidence needed to answer
> the research question or support the next decision.

---

## 1. Define the Research Question

Before searching broadly, establish:

- the question to answer;
- why the answer matters;
- required freshness;
- relevant scope;
- what evidence would materially change the next decision.

Avoid open-ended collection when a narrower question can resolve the task.

If the question itself depends on unresolved user intent, use the established
alignment path before performing expensive research.

---

## 2. Prefer Authoritative Sources

Choose source type according to the claim.

Prefer, when available:

- primary official documentation;
- original filings or datasets;
- governing standards or specifications;
- first-party announcements for first-party facts;
- original research for research claims.

Use high-quality secondary sources for:

- context;
- synthesis;
- independent interpretation;
- discovery of primary evidence.

Use community or anecdotal sources when the question concerns experience,
sentiment, practice, or issues not captured by formal documentation.

Do not treat source prestige as a substitute for relevance to the specific
claim.

---

## 3. Check Freshness and Version

For facts that can change, establish the relevant date, version, release,
period, or effective state.

Treat stale information as historical evidence, not current truth.

When a source lacks an obvious date or version, reduce confidence or seek a more
traceable source when the distinction matters.

Do not perform freshness ceremony for stable facts whose date cannot affect the
answer.

---

## 4. Search for Evidence, Not Agreement

Do not stop at the first source that supports the expected answer.

For consequential or uncertain questions, seek evidence that can:

- confirm the claim;
- contradict it;
- narrow its scope;
- reveal an exception;
- show that two sources describe different periods or definitions.

The goal is not an artificial source count. One decisive primary source can be
stronger than many derivative summaries.

---

## 5. Separate Source Claims From Inference

Keep distinct:

- what a source explicitly establishes;
- what is reasonably derived from multiple established facts;
- what remains interpretation or hypothesis.

Do not attribute an inference to a source that did not make it.

When quoting or paraphrasing, preserve material qualifiers and scope.

---

## 6. Resolve Contradictions Deliberately

When sources disagree, investigate whether the difference comes from:

- date or version;
- definition;
- scope;
- measurement method;
- primary vs derivative reporting;
- correction or later update;
- genuine unresolved disagreement.

Do not average contradictory claims into a false compromise.

If the conflict cannot be resolved, preserve it explicitly and report what would
be needed to settle it.

---

## 7. Bound Research Cost

Continue research while additional evidence has a reasonable chance of
materially changing confidence or the next decision.

Stop when:

- the question is sufficiently answered for its purpose;
- independent evidence converges;
- the remaining uncertainty is immaterial;
- the next useful evidence is disproportionately costly or unavailable.

Do not research adjacent topics merely because they are interesting.

---

## 8. Preserve Provenance

For material claims, preserve enough source identity to allow later checking.

Depending on the medium, this may include:

- citation;
- URL or document identifier;
- filing period;
- page, section, or line;
- publication or effective date;
- dataset version.

Do not create citation noise for trivial statements, but do not detach material
facts from their evidence.

---

## 9. Research Output

A useful research packet normally separates:

### Established facts
Claims strongly supported by the retrieved evidence.

### Qualified or disputed findings
Claims whose scope, source quality, or evidence remains limited.

### Source notes
Only provenance and freshness information needed for later use.

### Gaps
Missing evidence that could materially affect the next step.

### Research boundary
What was intentionally not investigated.

Do not turn the research output into a final recommendation unless decision
synthesis is also required and authorized.

---

## Completion Rule

Research is sufficient when the next stage can distinguish established facts,
source-limited claims, inference, and material unknowns without needing to
repeat the same evidence search.

The objective is:

**decision-relevant evidence with explicit provenance, freshness, and
uncertainty.**
